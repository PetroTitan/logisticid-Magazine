"Two pallets, Poland to Germany, what does it cost?"

This is a reasonable-looking question that cannot be answered. Not because the sender was careless — the omitted details are not obviously important unless you have seen what happens when they are wrong.

A forwarder receiving that message can guess, quote against the guess, and revise the quote when the guess turns out wrong. Or they can ask. Either way the information gets collected; the only variable is whether it happens before or after a number has been given and remembered.

## What is actually being priced

A road freight quote is an estimate of what a carrier will require to perform a specific movement. That requires knowing what space the cargo occupies, what equipment moves it, what the vehicle must do at each end, and when it must happen.

Each item below maps to one of those. That is why vague answers cost money later: they do not remove the requirement, they defer it.

## The cargo

**Dimensions and weight, measured.** Length, width and height of each item or pallet, and the weight of each. Not the weight of the goods — the gross weight, including the pallet and packaging, since that is what is lifted and what counts against a vehicle's limits.

**Whether it can be stacked.** This one item can change which service is possible at all. A pallet that cannot take weight on top occupies its footprint for the full internal height of the vehicle, not just its own height. A consignment described as "two pallets" that turns out to be two non-stackable pallets can consume the space of four.

**Packaging and unit type.** Standard pallet, oversized pallet, crate, drum, loose items, on a frame. This determines handling equipment and whether the load is a standard network unit.

**What the goods are.** Not for curiosity: the commodity affects acceptance, sharing, security, temperature requirements and whether specific regimes apply. A general description is enough at quote stage — but it should be accurate.

**Whether anything is regulated.** Dangerous goods, temperature-controlled cargo, excise goods, waste and goods under specific customs regimes are handled under their own rules.

:::boundary
If the cargo may be classified as dangerous goods, that classification is not something to settle from an article or a general description. It is determined under the applicable dangerous-goods rules, and it changes packaging, documentation, vehicle, driver and route requirements. Raise it explicitly at quote stage and let it be assessed properly.
:::

## The two ends

The vehicle has to physically reach and work at both locations, and the constraints there are frequently the difference between a quote that holds and one that does not.

**Full addresses, with postcodes.** A lane priced city-to-city can change materially once the actual sites are known.

**Site type and access.** Loading dock, ground level, forklift on site, tail lift required, restricted approach, height or weight limits, city access restrictions. A site that cannot take a standard trailer needs a different vehicle, and that is a different price rather than a surcharge on the same one.

**Loading and unloading responsibility.** Whether the driver is expected to assist, and whether either site requires booking a time slot.

**Opening hours and booking requirements.** A vehicle arriving outside a receiving window waits, and waiting has a cost.

## The timing

**When the goods are genuinely ready.** Ready means loadable — produced, packed, labelled, documented and physically available. "Ready Monday" meaning "packing finishes Monday afternoon" is a different collection day.

**How firm the delivery requirement is.** There is a real difference between a preference, a target and a date with a contractual or operational consequence, and it changes which service is appropriate. Stating it accurately is more useful than stating it urgently.

**Whether it repeats.** A regular flow is a different commercial conversation from a one-off, and worth mentioning even when the first shipment is a trial.

## The commercial frame

**Who arranges and pays for which leg**, under whatever incoterm or commercial arrangement applies. This determines what is being quoted at all.

**Whether customs formalities are involved.** Within the EU customs single market most road movements do not require them; movements to, from or through non-EU territory generally do.

:::boundary
Which customs procedures apply to a specific movement, and who is responsible for them, is a question for a customs broker or the relevant customs authority. It depends on the goods, the route, the parties and the applicable regime, and getting it wrong has consequences at the border rather than in a spreadsheet.
:::

**Cargo value, where insurance matters.** Carrier liability for international road carriage is generally limited by the applicable convention and is not the same as the value of the goods. If the difference matters for a consignment, it is worth raising at quote stage rather than discovering after an incident.

## A worked contrast

Not actionable:

> Two pallets, Poland to Germany, next week, how much?

Actionable:

> 2 pallets, each 120 × 80 × 145 cm, 420 kg gross each, non-stackable. Packaged machine components, nothing regulated. Collection from Wrocław 50-XXX, ground level, forklift available, open 07:00–15:00. Delivery to Leipzig 04-XXX, loading dock, slot booking required. Ready from Tuesday, delivery needed by Friday — production line waiting. EU to EU, no customs. Recurring roughly monthly if it works.

The second is not much longer. It can be priced without assumptions, which is the whole difference.

## Why this is worth the effort

A quote built on assumptions is provisional whether or not it is labelled that way. When an assumption fails — the pallets do not stack, the site has no forklift, the goods are ready two days later — the quote changes, and it changes at the point where a shipper has already committed to a number.

Complete information at the start does not merely produce a faster answer. It produces one that survives contact with the loading bay.

:::boundary
This describes what a forwarder generally needs in order to quote. It is not a determination of what your shipment requires, what it will cost or whether a carrier will accept it. Those depend on the cargo, the lane, equipment availability, timing, applicable rules and the carrier's own acceptance criteria.
:::
